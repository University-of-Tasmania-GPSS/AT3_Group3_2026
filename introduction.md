# Background

Water. We all know its importance to life, how critical it is to keep it clean and contamination free for human, non-human and environmental health. But have you ever thought about what happens to it under the ground? There are many places in Tasmania where water has turned the subterranean depths into a sparkling, mysterious, stunningly beautiful wonderland, silent except for the musical dripping of water or the gurgling of the crystal clear streams. In other places there are waterfalls and underground rivers flowing into the dark where only experienced cave divers dare to go.

## Rock that rain dissolves

Most landscapes filter water as it moves through soil and rock. Karst can do the opposite. Rain dissolves limestone, gradually opening cracks into sinkholes, caves and underground streams.

Water entering through a sinkhole can bypass much of the filtration and attenuation that occurs in soil and porous material before reaching a cave, spring or aquifer. At Mole Creek, surface run-off is captured quickly by openings in the rock and passes through conduits with little cleaning (Eberhard & Houshold, 2002). Whatever the water picks up on the way goes with it.

## Small, scattered, easy to overlook

There is about 4,100 km² of karst mapped in Tasmania, which covers 6% of the state, in patches from the north-west to the south. It is a small share of the land, but it carries the springs and wet ecosystems that depend on it.

We start at Mole Creek, a well-studied karst area in northern Tasmania where caves, sinkholes and active land use bring the problem into focus. What happens on the land above matters here: what falls on the surface can enter the karst below.

## What exactly are we calling vulnerability?

Karst vulnerability is the ease with which something at the surface can enter and move through the groundwater system. It depends on both the landscape and the pathways that connect the surface to groundwater. Because those factors vary across a landscape, vulnerability is commonly represented as a spatial index: several characteristics are mapped, scored and combined to produce a relative measure of vulnerability.

**Method:** A systematic review of karst vulnerability methods highlights scale and parameter transferability as key dimensions separating them (Iván & Mádl-Szőnyi, 2017). Tested against real tracer data, EPIK and COP disagree, and EPIK tends to overestimate vulnerability (Ravbar & Goldscheider, 2009).

**Scale:** Parameters tuned at one karst system do not automatically transfer to another (Moreno-Gómez et al., 2019). Fine hydrological detail, such as 2 m flow-routing, only works at a small site, which forces a trade-off with statewide coverage.

**Data:** Data scarcity is itself a core limitation of karst vulnerability mapping (Ollivier et al., 2019). Free statewide land cover (DEA) cannot tell a pine plantation from native forest. Tasmania's own LIST layer can, but it is a heavier, Tasmania-only dataset.
